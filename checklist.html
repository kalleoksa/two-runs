AI in design & development · run sheet · v1

Take this into account now, and the next stage costs less.

A checklist for running the assisted process on a real project, stage by stage. It is written for the first time through, when the order of the work is unfamiliar and the expensive mistakes are the ones nobody warns you about. Each item says what it prevents.

Every claim about what an item prevents comes from the model in the two-runs demo, which is invented rather than measured — the problem ids below match its record table, so you can see where each one was caught and what it was priced at. Items marked authored are not in that model at all: they are practice, and they are the ones to argue with first.

How to use it. Work down the stage you are in. Nothing here is a gate you cannot walk through — but the model prices every skipped item, and it charges most for the ones skipped early.

The two stages with a decision in them have a box instead of a list. Both options are real choices; what matters is knowing which stage you have moved the problems to.

Print it if that is easier. The boxes are for ticking.

Upstream · deciding what is true and what is wanted

0

Value hypothesis

product manager decides
  • Write down what would make you stop, while nobody is invested in continuing yet. Authored
  • Name the measures that must not get worse, not only the one you are trying to move. Authored
  • Decide whether AI belongs in this piece of work at all, separately from whether the work is worth doing. Authored

This stage prevents nothing in the model's problem list — the only stage on this sheet where that is true. What it buys is the ability to stop, which the model does not price and a first project usually discovers it needed.

1

Scope

product manager with tech lead
  • Write the not-building list down instead of leaving it understood. It is the list an AI tool will otherwise cheerfully build against later. Authored
  • Name who is out, not only what: annual plans, enterprise, partner-billed, second members on a family plan. Authored
2

Research

designers own it
  • Read a hand sample against whatever the tool clustered, before anybody quotes the counts in a meeting. Authored
  • Write down what you still do not know, as a list, and carry it into the next stage rather than into somebody's memory. Authored
3

Concept & probe

the stage that decides the rest
  • Enumerate the situations against the domain model, not against the flow you have just designed. Prevents D4 promo lock-in state missing · D5 collections state missing
  • Ask which states nobody in the room has ever met, then go looking for them in the data instead of in the team's memory. Prevents D4 promo lock-in state missing · D5 collections state missing
  • Decide what happens to each prototype before it starts to look finished. Prevents D9 prototype fidelity debt shipped
  • Put the surviving list of situations somewhere the next two stages will actually read. Authored

The heaviest stage on this sheet. Two of the model's problems are created here, and the distance between catching them here and meeting them after release is most of the gap the demo shows. Neither is exotic: a customer part-way through a promotional price, and an account already in debt collection.

4

Decide

a human decides, and signs it
  • Write the decision, the reason, and what was rejected — the reason is the part someone will ask for months later. Authored
  • Write down what the decision leaves open. Every open question here becomes somebody's guess at a later stage. Prevents D6 cancellation effective date ambiguous

Worth knowing: in the model, D6 is created at this stage by not deciding rather than by deciding wrongly. It is the cheapest problem on the list to prevent and one of the more expensive ones to meet.

Stage 4 · Decide · what each option commits you to

The prototypes exist and the build starts next.

Keep one
Then list every stand-in it contains and replace them before release. The model charges keeping it as a problem that reaches customers on every level of specification, because pretend behaviour is invisible once real code surrounds it.
Delete them
Then check that the written spec carries what the prototype was carrying. Once they are gone, nothing else holds the detail that only existed as clicking.

Both are defensible. What is not defensible is keeping one without a list of what in it was fake.

The hinge · one decision, three readers

5

Specify

product manager owns coherence
  • For the team: every flow, every state including the ugly ones, the words on screen, the accessibility requirements. Prevents D7 focus management on interrupt · D8 copy fails legal tone review
  • For the tools: the data exactly as the live system returns it, plus an explicit list of what must not be touched. Prevents D1 subscription contract mismatch · D2 invented plan status enum
  • For the tests: the situations that survived stage 3, written as cases that either pass or fail. Prevents D3 timezone at billing period boundary
  • If you cannot write the tool-facing one, stop and go back. That is information about whether the upstream work is finished, not a documentation problem. Authored
  • Review all three together, in one sitting, with the people who own each one. If they disagree with each other, the disagreement is the finding. Authored
Stage 5 · how much gets written down · what each option commits you to

Whatever is not written down here gets decided later by whoever happens to be typing.

Notes only
Cheapest at this stage. Expect build, test and review effort to rise, and expect the problems above to be met at test or after release rather than here.
Written for the team
Expect the team-facing problems to be caught, and the tool-facing and test-facing ones to surface while building and testing.
All three readers
The most expensive option at this stage, and the one where the model catches the most before anything is built.

The demo prices all three, and you can change every assumption behind them. The only position this sheet takes: whichever you choose, know which stage you have moved the problems to, and tell the people who will be standing there.

Downstream · execution against a written target

6

Build

developers direct; tech lead reviews
  • Book the review time before the work starts, as time in a calendar rather than as goodwill. Without it the reviewer becomes the queue and the speed goes back out. Authored
  • Keep the money arithmetic human, and review it twice. It is the one place where an answer that looks plausible is not good enough. Authored
  • Check generated work against the live service, not against the mock it was written with. Prevents D1 subscription contract mismatch · D2 invented plan status enum
  • Read migrations and anything else destructive in full before running it. Authored
7

Test

QA owns what counts as correct
  • Run everything a rule can check on every change, so the human time goes where rules cannot reach. Authored
  • Test by hand in the two places rules do not help: the money path, and the whole flow on a screen reader. Prevents D7 focus management on interrupt
  • When you accept a problem instead of fixing it, write down that you accepted it and why. Authored

A gap worth knowing about: the demo's model has no category for a problem that is found, priced and knowingly shipped, though that is the most common outcome for accessibility and minor defects. Everything it counts is either caught or escaped, which flatters whichever process finds things earlier. Your record should have the third category even though the model does not.

8

Release & measure

product manager owns the read
  • Release to a slice of customers before all of them. Authored
  • Read the target next to the measures that must not get worse, never on its own. A flow can post excellent completion numbers while quietly generating refund complaints. Authored
9

Record

the stage everyone skips
  • Write what you handed to a tool, what came back wrong, and how long the reviewing actually took against what you expected. Authored
  • Put the conventions you had to explain twice back into the repository, where the tools will read them next time. Authored
  • Record what this sheet got wrong on your project. Authored

The model treats this as the only stage that makes the next project cheaper than this one, and every real team skips it. If one item on this sheet is going to be dropped, the model's own numbers argue against it being this stage.

Where the AI route stops paying

The model has a crossover, and it is the most useful thing in it. Specifying thinly raises build, test and review effort and moves the catches later; combine that with keeping a prototype and the assisted run comes out costing more than the conventional route in the same model, with more problems reaching customers. The demo states which paths cross that line under whatever assumptions you enter, and you can move every one of them.

The practical reading for a first project: the saving is not in the generating. It is in what you wrote down before you generated. Every item on this sheet that prevents something is upstream of the build.

What this cannot tell you

Every "prevents" above is a claim from an invented model, not an observation, and the problem list it draws on is a plausible reconstruction rather than a record. The items marked authored are not even in that model — they are practice, and the person to correct them is someone who has delivered this way rather than someone who has read the documents.

A sheet like this is worth what the first project spends proving it wrong. Keep the list of what it missed, and bring it back here.
